Cluckwork · #941 expanded lay-rate chart

The Lay rate card can show fourteen daily bars and no more — 22px slots in 342px of phone plot (#912). A ninety-day range does not fit in a dashboard card, and the answer to that is not thinner bars. It is a second surface.

Turn the lamp down and put the whole ledger on the wall.

Three ways to build that surface, all composed like the post-login brand splash: a fixed full-viewport backdrop, the chart centred on both axes, and one way out underneath it. The difference between them is what happens to ninety bars once they are there — scroll past them, shrink them to fit, or keep a readable window and a map of the rest.

What every concept keeps

Missing, partial and complete stay three distinct marks: a solid bar, a hatched bar that reads as a floor rather than a figure, and no bar at all. The reserved readout row stays reserved, so selecting a day reflows nothing. The strip stays one tab stop with Left/Right/Home/End inside it (#654/#777/#780) — ninety tab stops would be worse than fourteen, not better. Dates stay farm-local. Every string is shown in en, es and tl.

Concepts

A · Splash frame, daily bars, horizontal scroll

  • Geometry: #912's own slot size, uncapped. 22px slots, 4px gaps on desktop and 2px on a phone. 90 days is 2,340px of strip; about 45 of them are on screen at 1280 and 14 on a phone.
  • Accessibility: arrow keys walk the strip and scroll the selected day into view, so the keyboard never loses the selection off-screen. Home/End reach the ends of a 90-day range in one press.
  • Touch: drag is the native gesture for a scroller and every slot stays at its measured 22px hit width.
  • Build: cheapest of the three. DayStrip renders as-is inside an overflow container; the new parts are the axis gutter, the scroll cue and scroll-into-view on arrow keys.
  • Conflict: #914 banned horizontal scroll inside the card. This is not the card. The ban's reason — a scroller hidden inside a dashboard panel that nobody discovers — does not survive on a surface whose whole subject is one chart.
  • Weakness: you never see all ninety days at once. Answered by the Daily/Weekly toggle in the head, not by a second concept.

B · Splash frame, fit the whole range

  • Geometry: the strip always fills the column. 90 days over ~1,090px of desktop plot is a 12px slot; the same 90 over 300px of phone plot is a 3px slot.
  • Accessibility: keyboard is unaffected and arguably better — nothing moves under the selection.
  • Touch: this is where it fails. A 3px target is not tappable, so the phone must fall back to weekly bars, which is the collapsed card again with more padding.
  • Build: also cheap — it is the existing flex strip with the cap lifted — plus the auto-fallback rule and its wording.
  • Conflict: none with #914; it is #914's own weekly answer applied to a bigger box.
  • Weakness: answers "what is the shape of the quarter" well and "what happened on 14 August" not at all.

C · Overview map + scrolling window Chosen

  • Owner decision, 2026-09-24: C, with the window made a real horizontal scroller, and the expanded view following the app theme rather than forcing night tokens. The captures below the card are the C2-* set; the older C-* frames are the pre-decision draft and are kept only as history.
  • Geometry: two plots of the same range. A 42px map (34px on a phone) that always fits, and beneath it concept A's strip verbatim — #912's 22px slots in a native overflow-x:auto region. 90 days is 2,340px of strip; about 41 of them are on screen at 1280 and 12 on a phone.
  • Sync: one writer each way. Scrolling moves the box; pressing or dragging the map scrolls the window. See the note under the captures.
  • Accessibility: the strip stays one tab stop with Left/Right/Home/End inside it, and the selection scrolls itself into view. Page Up/Down and the pager buttons move the window without moving the selection. The map is role="img": it is a picture of where you are, never the only way to get there.
  • Touch: the window is swiped — the native gesture for a scroller, with every slot at its measured 22px hit width. The map takes a tap to jump; at 390 it is deliberately not a drag handle.
  • Build: A's cost plus the map. The scroller, the edge cue, the axis gutter and scroll-into-view are A. The new parts are one more strip, two lines of scroll→box arithmetic, the pointer handler, and Page Up/Down.
  • Conflict: #914 banned horizontal scroll inside the card. This is not the card, and unlike A it is not a bare scroller either: the map shows where the clipped days are before anyone has to discover that scrolling exists.
  • Weakness: still a control nothing else in Cluckwork has, and the map inherits the series' flatness — it shows where nobody filed and little else.

Decided: C, made scrollable and theme-following.

The lab originally recommended A. The owner chose C on 2026-09-24 and settled the open question underneath it at the same time: the expanded view is light in light mode and dark in dark mode, using the app's own tokens, not a forced lightbox. The frame is otherwise unchanged from the splash — fixed full-viewport backdrop, centred column, one way out that takes focus, fade only under prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference.

What that buys over plain A is discovery and placement: A's scroller tells you there is more only by fading at its edge, and answers "where am I in the quarter" only in the footer's prose. C answers both with a picture. What it costs is the map — and the map is where the build delta lives, so the note below prices it.

How the two-way sync works

There is exactly one writer in each direction, and that is the whole design:

scroller → map. The scroller's scroll event calls updateBox(), which writes only box.style.left and box.style.width, both derived from scrollLeft, clientWidth and scrollWidth. No state, no index, no re-render.
map → scroller. A pointer press on the map computes a fraction of its width and writes only scroller.scrollLeft, centring the window on the pressed point. Dragging repeats that on pointermove under pointer capture, so the sweep survives the cursor leaving the 42px strip.

Why this cannot oscillate. A programmatic scrollLeft fires scroll, which repaints the box; the box never writes scrollLeft back, so the chain terminates after one hop. No guard flag, no rAF latch, no epsilon comparison. The feedback loop everyone writes by accident is the other shape — the one where the box owns a start index and the strip is re-rendered from it. That is what this lab's first concept C did, which is precisely why it could not scroll: the map and the window each believed they owned the position. If a future change gives the box its own state, that loop comes back. Keep the scroller as the single source of truth for "where am I", and the map as a projection of it.

One exception is worth knowing before it is rediscovered. A strip that fits still reports a few pixels of scrollWidth over clientWidth, because the date rule's end label overhangs its slot. Treated as scrollable, that offsets the box by a couple of percent and lights the edge cue on a chart with nothing clipped. Both updateBox() and updateCues() answer the fits case from the no-scroll class instead of from the measurement.

One thing the scrolling version changed underneath

Keyboard entry has to follow the window, in two places. With nothing selected, the strip's key handler fell back to index 0, so the first Left or Right press selected the first day of the range. Worse, the strip is one tab stop and that stop is rendered on index 0 — so merely tabbing into a window scrolled to September made the browser scroll 25 June back into view, the chart losing your place before a key was pressed. Both now resolve to the first day actually on screen, computed from scrollLeft: moveTabStopIntoView() runs on every scroll, and the key handler uses the same index as its fallback. Verified by tabbing in at scrollLeft 1,173 and pressing Left five times — the selection lands on 8 August and the window does not move. Concept A carries the same latent bug and the same fix; it is only visible once the window can be far from the start of the range.

What happens at 30 days

At 1280 it does not need to scroll. Thirty days at a 22px slot and a 4px gap is 780px inside 1,076px of plot, so the strip fits: the scroller takes no-scroll, the strip fills the column, the pager buttons disable, both edge cues go dark, the footer reads All 30 days shown, and the map box spans the whole map at reduced opacity — a position indicator saying "you are looking at everything" (C2-1280-30-light.png, C2-1280-30-dark.png). The break-even at 1280 is about 41 days, so 30 fits and 60 does not.

Two consequences worth deciding before implementation rather than after. First, filling the column stretches each slot to about 29px, wider than #912's 22 — the alternative, leaving a third of the plot empty on the right, looks broken, so this is the right trade, but the chart at 30 days is visibly not the chart at 90. Second, on a phone nothing fits: 30 days is 720px against roughly 300px of plot, so the phone always scrolls and the map is always live. The fits case is a desktop-only state, and any test that asserts a disabled pager must pin a viewport.

Touch on the phone

Swiping the window is the gesture. It is a native scroller, so momentum, rubber-band and the platform's own overlay scrollbar all come free, and every slot keeps its measured 22px hit width — the reason C does not shrink bars to fit. The map is 34px tall at 390 and the box, at twelve days of ninety, is about 44px wide: large enough to read as a position, too small to grab reliably beside a 390px viewport. So the phone gets tap to jump and no drag: pointerdown centres the window on the tapped point and the handler returns before wiring pointermove. The map carries touch-action:none so that tap is not first interpreted as a page pan.

The one thing to watch in implementation: the window and the page behind it both scroll, in different axes. The veil is position:fixed and the page beneath it is inert, so a vertical drag inside the panel has nothing to scroll and a horizontal one reaches the strip — but that only holds while the panel itself fits the viewport. If the panel ever grows taller than a phone screen, the two scrollers compete and a diagonal swipe becomes ambiguous. Cap the plot height, as the frame already does, rather than letting the column scroll.

Build-cost delta over plain A

A is the floor: put DayStrip in an overflow-x container, add the fixed axis gutter, the edge cue and scroll-into-view on arrow keys. Everything C adds sits on top of an unchanged A:

The map — a second, unselectable render of the same periods at a fixed height, plus its label row. Reuses the scale and the three marks; no new data path.
The box — one absolutely-positioned element and the four lines of updateBox().
The pointer handler — press-to-centre, plus drag under pointer capture on non-phone widths. This is the only genuinely new interaction, and it is about fifteen lines with no library.
Page Up / Page Down and the pager buttons — scrollLeft += ±clientWidth, shared by all three entry points.

Call it a day's work on top of A, and no new dependency: the earlier estimate of "dearest, a brush control MUI gives you nothing for" priced a brush — a control that owns a range and re-renders the plot beneath it. This is not that. The scroller owns the range, natively, and the map only draws it. The part that stays expensive is not code but explanation: two plots of the same data, one of them uninspectable, is still a thing a farm manager has to be taught once.

Three things the renders showed that the plan did not

A longer range does not produce a more interesting shape. A laying flock's daily output varies by single-digit percent, and these bars are anchored at zero on purpose (#654 — a cropped axis would dramatise a 3% swing). So the complete-day average sits within a few percent of the peak at 30, 60 and 90 days alike, and ninety bars are ninety bars of nearly the same height. What the long range makes legible is where the gaps and the partial days are, and how long the September heat dip took to come back. Say that in the issue before it ships, not after someone opens it expecting a curve.

Concept C's overview inherits that flatness. Look at its 42px map: it shows where nobody filed, and almost nothing else. A map earns its build cost on a series with shape, and this one has little. It still earns it here, but on placement rather than on pattern — it answers where in the quarter am I, not what shape is the quarter.

The 7-day preset draws thin bars in wide slots. With the cap at 14, seven days gives each slot a seventh of the card and the bar stays capped at 18px, so the card fills with track rather than bar (capture A-1280-collapsed-7day.png). Not this issue's problem — visible only because 7 is now a preset — but worth a line on #941 rather than a rediscovery later.

On theme

Settled on 2026-09-24: C follows the app's own setting. Light page, light expanded view; dark page, dark one — the app's tokens either way, no new palette. Concepts A and B are left as drawn, with data-theme="dark" forced on the veil root, so the lab still shows what the rejected lightbox looked like.

The lightbox had a real argument: the dashboard goes out, one lit panel remains, and closing returns the daylit page. What decided against it is that a light-mode farm would meet a surface that matches nothing else it has ever seen in the app, on a press of a control labelled only Expand chart. Following the theme never surprises anyone, and it costs the view nothing — the frame still does the separating work, because it is a full-viewport backdrop in --canvas with the chart lifted off it in --surface under --shadow-dialog. In light mode that reads as cream-on-cream with a raised card, which is how every other dialog in the app already reads.

Two things the light captures showed that the dark-only draft could not. The map box is drawn in --ink, so it is near-black on a cream map in light and near-white on an aubergine one in dark — the same element, the same contrast, in both directions; it needs the 2px rule and the 1px --surface halo to hold that on the phone at 44px wide, and it has them. And the keyboard focus ring is the weak spot in both themes rather than only one: --focus is --stat-accent while a selected bar is filled with --ink, so the ring lands accent-on-ink and is far quieter than a focus ring should be (compare C2-1280-90-kbd-light.png with C2-1280-90-readout-light.png — the difference is one thin purple line). That is inherited from #654's selected-bar treatment, not introduced here, but the expanded view is where a keyboard user will spend the most time on this chart, so it should be fixed with the slice rather than after it.

The close path still matters even without the dark-always rule: fade the veil out, do not unmount it under a repaint. Same 0.2s, same prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference guard as the splash. And returning focus to the Expand control is not optional — it is the only thing that tells a screen-reader user where the view went.
